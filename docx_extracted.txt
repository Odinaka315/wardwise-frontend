The situation
Four of you have been employed as Programme Analyst, Systems Analyst, Computer Engineer and Data Processing Officer at the Information Management Unit (IMU/ICT) of the Federal Neuro-Psychiatric Hospital, Yaba. The hospital runs 362 inpatient beds across nine wards, about 128 short of about 490 beds, a day hospital of thirty places, a busy outpatient department, a crisis and triage unit, and four community teams that cover twenty-two catchment areas across Lagos and beyond.
The management board has a problem it cannot describe in the language of a general hospital. Patients do not simply arrive, get treated and leave. They move between the acute ward, the rehabilitation ward, the day hospital and community follow-up, and they keep moving until something ends the process. A patient recovers and is discharged from the service. A patient is transferred elsewhere. A patient dies. Or, most often of all, a patient stops coming and is lost to follow-up, which the wards call the silent discharge.
Medical Directors, beginning from Dr Idowu Malomo, Dr HTO Ladapo, Dr RA Lawal, Dr R Adebayo, Dr OC Ogun and currently, Dr OA Owoeye, have all put this exact question this way:
“Every month we admit, treat and discharge, and every month the same faces return. Given the beds, staff and money we already have, what is the best course of action?”
Your set (four of you) must answer with evidence. What the board is looking for is a dashoard with actionable insight and a decision, and it wants to know what the decision assumes.
The absorbing view of the service
Everything in this project is built on one structure. The care pathway has five transient states and five absorbing states.
Transient states
Acute inpatient
Rehabilitation inpatient
Day hospital
Outpatient follow-up
Community relapse
Absorbing states
Recovered and discharged from the service
Lost to follow-up
Transferred to another facility
Long stay institutional care
Deceased
The fourth of those needs saying plainly. The long term care ward holds people who entered years or decades ago and now live in the hospital under a federal continuing care grant. In service-planning terms that state is absorbing. Once a patient enters it, the bed is committed and the realistic exits are death or, occasionally, a family member deciding to take the person home. Death is by far the more common of the two.
Treat it as absorbing in the main chain, because that is what it means for capacity. Then model the exit separately from long_stay_residents, which carries sixty-two residents with year of entry, guardianship status, next-of-kin contact, nursing hours and the exits observed over the three years. A survival model on that register gives you the exit hazard, and the exit hazard is what tells the board when a long term bed will next become available. Answer that question explicitly, because it is the only bed-release forecast the ward has ever been asked for.
The table care_pathway_transitions holds monthly moves for 3,200 patients over three years. From that table you must build the transition matrix, split it into the transient block Q and the absorption block R, and compute the fundamental matrix N = (I − Q)⁻¹. Three quantities follow, and each one answers a management question.
The row sums of N give the expected number of months a patient spends in the system before absorption, starting from each state. This is the true length of care, not the length of stay.
The entries of N give the expected number of months spent in each state before absorption. This is what a ward actually costs the service over a patient's career.
The matrix B = NR gives the probability of ending in each absorbing state. Compare recovery against loss to follow-up, and compare both against long stay institutional care, then explain what those comparisons mean for a service that measures itself by discharges.
Do this before you build anything else. Every later model in the project takes its parameters, its states or its objective from this structure.
What the board question contains
The single question hides six, and each maps to a discipline in your training. Answer them in order, because each answer feeds the next.
1. What has happened?
Profile demand, occupancy, stay, cost, attendance, safety incidents and community workload across the three years. Quantify the seasonal shape, and explain the July 2024 dip and the December pattern. Use descriptive statistics, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, correlation and index numbers. The psychotropic price series needs Laspeyres, Paasche and Fisher indices, because the naira did not sit still.
Look hard at occupancy by unit. Two wards run above capacity for most of the period, two sit largely idle, and one is full on every single day of the three years without ever turning over. Distinguish nominal capacity from effective capacity, because fifty beds are permanently spoken for and no admission policy can touch them. None of this is announced anywhere in the data.
2. What will happen?
Forecast daily admissions, hourly crisis unit arrivals and monthly demand for the ten highest-value pharmacy lines. Compare at least four approaches drawn from ARIMA, exponential smoothing, XGBoost and LSTM. Report MAE, RMSE, MAPE and adjusted R-squared, and name the model you would put into production.
3. Who is at risk, and who resembles whom?
Build a classifier for ninety-day readmission and a second for clinic non-attendance. Report accuracy, precision, recall, F1, specificity, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and log loss. Then segment the patient population without any label, and defend your choice of cluster count using the silhouette score, the Davies-Bouldin index and the Calinski-Harabasz index. Only after that may you touch the hidden label, and only to compute the adjusted Rand index and normalised mutual information.
Add a third and a fourth target that matter more than either. Predict which patients will be absorbed into loss to follow-up. Build it from v_absorption_summary joined to patient circumstances, and treat family support, distance and travel fare as first-class features rather than controls. Then predict which rehabilitation ward patients are on a trajectory towards long stay institutional care, using time in state, symptom trajectory, guardianship and family contact. That prediction is the only one the hospital could act on early enough to change the outcome.
4. What pattern does the flow follow under uncertainty?
Estimate the absorbing chain above. Test whether a first-order Markov assumption holds, or whether sojourn time in a state changes the transition probabilities, which would push you towards a semi-Markov treatment. Fit an arrival process to the crisis unit and test the Poisson assumption honestly.
5. What happens if we change something?
Build a discrete-event simulation of the journey from crisis presentation through ward and day hospital into community follow-up, with relapse feeding back to the front door. Validate it against observed census, waiting times and absorption proportions before you trust it, then run the scenarios below.
6. How should our decisions learn as conditions change?
Derive a placement policy from placement_decision_log, compare it against the threshold rule the hospital uses now, and state the conditions under which your policy would fail.
The thesis model chain
This capstone follows the an architecture, and the point of the exercise is that you learn to build the systems design way. The rule is simple.
One named core model carries the decision. Every other technique is an input generator for it, not a rival to it.
Statistics, forecasting, classification, clustering and simulation do not sit beside the core model as equal deliverables. They produce its parameters: the transition matrix, the demand distributions, the risk scores, the cost coefficients, the service rates. The core model then produces the recommendation, and the recommendation is what the board sees.
Each team takes one core model, drawn from the three architectures in my thesis. The full specification and notation for each will be issued separately with the thesis extract, so use the names as given.

Evaluation of the core model
Classical hypothesis testing belongs to the descriptive stage in question one, and it stays there. The core model is not judged by a p-value but on three criteria, and your report must address each explicitly.
Data quality. State what each parameter rests on, how many observations support it, and how much it would move under the missingness and outliers you found. Parameters estimated from thin cells must be flagged as thin.
Model chain consistency. Show that the units, time bases and state definitions match at every junction of the chain. A monthly transition matrix feeding a daily simulation is an error, not a detail. Show that a change in an upstream input propagates sensibly to the recommendation.
Usefulness of the decision support output. Show that the output answers a question the hospital actually asks, in a form a clinician or an administrator can act on. A policy table that no ward manager can read has failed this criterion however elegant its derivation.
The system you must build
The final deliverable should be a working web application that anyone on the board can open in a browser and use without your help. 
The five layers
Data layer. Oracle, Microsoft SQL Server, PostgreSQL or any R-DBMS of your choice, loaded from fnph_yaba_dataset.sql. The database is the single source of truth, and the teaching views in the dump are there to be used. No CSV files in the running system.
Prediction service. Trained models saved as artefacts and loaded at start-up. Nothing is trained inside a web request. Retraining is a scheduled or manually triggered job, and every artefact records the data window and the seed it came from.
Optimisation service. The core model, solved as a background job through a queue rather than in the request thread. A solve that takes four minutes must not hold a browser connection open. The user submits, the job runs, the screen polls and then shows the result.
Application programming interface. A documented HTTP interface between the back end and the front end. Publish an OpenAPI or equivalent specification. Every number the interface shows must come from an endpoint, not from a value typed into the front end.
Front end. A browser interface with the screens listed below, charts that updates when filters change, and honest loading and error states. It must be usable on a laptop in a meeting room and readable on a phone.
Stack
Use your preferred framework, your preferred R-DBMS, PostgreSQL, a job queue such as Celery with Redis, and a JavaScript front end such as React, Vue, Svelte. Solve with PuLP, Pyomo or OR-Tools against CBC or HiGHS.

Rules of the game
The data carries deliberate faults. Missing values sit in three columns of admissions, and twenty length-of-stay values are inflated. Find them, decide, and record the decision. Silent deletion costs marks.
Two columns are answer keys. patients.segment_label may be used only after clustering is complete, to compute ARI and NMI. unit_quarterly_panel.true_efficiency_hidden may be used only to check your efficiency analysis at the end. Neither may enter any model as a feature.
Every number about the hospital must trace to a table, a query or a model output. If you cannot show where it came from, remove it.
Assumptions are expected. Undocumented assumptions are not.
This is synthetic data about a real category of patient. Write about people with respect, use clinical terms correctly, and avoid language that reduces a patient to a bed-day or a defaulter. The phrase you will need often is loss to follow-up, and it describes a failure of the service, not of the patient. The long term residents are people with names, histories and, in many cases, nobody left who visits. Write about them as residents of the hospital, never as blocked beds, whatever the optimisation model calls them.

A closing note
The hospital in this dataset is synthetic. However, the pattern is not. Across Nigerian mental health services, a large share of patients leave the system without ever being discharged from it, and the service usually finds out months later, when they return in crisis. Your job over six weeks is to show that this pattern can be measured, modelled and acted on, and that a better course of action can be defended with numbers.
